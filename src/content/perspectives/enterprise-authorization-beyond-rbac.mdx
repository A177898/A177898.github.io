Enterprise Architecture places significant value on reuse, and authorization is an area where consistent, reusable capability can create significant value.

Most authorization begins with a relatively simple model:

<ArchitectureFlow
  steps={['User', 'Role', 'Permission', 'Resource']}
  direction="horizontal"
  label="Simple authorization model"
/>

For many applications, that may be sufficient.

The complexity emerges when the business asks a more contextual question:

> **Can this user actually initiate this transaction?**

At that point, a permission such as `PAYMENT_INITIATOR` may no longer provide enough information to make the decision.

The real question may be:

> **Can this subject perform this action against this resource, for this organisation, using this account, for this amount, through this channel, under the applicable relationship, mandate and policy, at this point in time?**

That is when authorization stops being a simple lookup and becomes a **decision**.

That is the central idea of this perspective.

And one distinction is important from the outset:

> **This is not an argument that RBAC is obsolete.**

RBAC remains valuable for expressing broad responsibility. Enterprise authorization becomes more complex when access also depends on relationships, resources, transaction context, mandates, limits, policy and obligations.

## The central problem

Authorization often begins with a simple model.

Then business context introduces complexity.

Can this user initiate this payment?

Can they approve it?

Can they view this account?

Can they act on behalf of this organisation?

Attempting to encode all of those dimensions into roles eventually creates role proliferation:

```text
PAYMENT_APPROVER
PAYMENT_APPROVER_100K
PAYMENT_APPROVER_500K
PAYMENT_APPROVER_ACCOUNT_A
PAYMENT_APPROVER_INTERNATIONAL
```

At that point, roles are no longer simply describing responsibility.

They are beginning to encode policy.

> **That is an architectural warning sign.**

## 1. Authentication is not authorization

This distinction needs to be established early because conflating authentication and authorization creates unclear architectural responsibilities.

Authentication answers:

> **Who are you?**

Authorization answers:

> **What are you permitted to do in this specific context?**

A valid token does not establish that a transaction should be permitted.

Likewise, an identity provider establishing who someone is does not necessarily mean that the identity provider should determine every business authorization decision.

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Identity establishes the subject. Authorization evaluates whether that subject may perform a specific action against a specific resource under the current context.
</PrincipleCallout>

That separation matters.

It creates a clear distinction between proving identity and determining authority.

## 2. Roles are useful, but they are not enough

Roles remain an effective mechanism for representing broad responsibility.

For example:

```text
Viewer
Operator
Administrator
Initiator
Approver
Auditor
```

They prevent us from having to assign potentially thousands of individual permissions directly to each subject.

The difficulty emerges when authorization depends on additional dimensions:

- Which organisation?
- Which account?
- Which resource?
- What amount?
- What jurisdiction?
- What channel?
- What mandate?
- What relationship?
- Who initiated the transaction?
- Has stronger authentication occurred?

Trying to represent every possible combination through roles causes the role model to become increasingly complex.

Eventually, policy begins to appear inside role names.

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Roles should describe broad responsibility. Policies should determine whether that responsibility applies in a particular context.
</PrincipleCallout>

This allows roles to remain understandable while more dynamic authorization concerns are evaluated separately.

## 3. Authorization is a decision, not a lookup

This is the most important conceptual shift.

A basic permissions service may operate like this:

```text
User → Permissions[]
```

The application then asks:

```text
Does Permissions[] contain PAYMENT_APPROVE?
```

Enterprise authorization increasingly requires evaluation rather than lookup.

The question becomes:

```text
Can Subject S
perform Action A
on Resource R
under Context C?
```

This changes the architecture significantly.

Instead of asking applications to interpret increasingly complex authorization data themselves, we establish a capability responsible for consistently evaluating authority.

## 4. Separate assignment from evaluation

Entitlements answer:

> **What has been assigned?**

Authorization answers:

> **Is this action permitted now?**

The two are closely related, but they are not identical.

A subject may have been assigned an Approver role.

That does not necessarily mean that subject may approve every transaction.

The decision may still depend on:

- resource scope;
- monetary limits;
- mandates;
- segregation of duties;
- jurisdiction;
- transaction state;
- risk;
- authentication strength.

An entitlement repository can tell us what authority has been assigned.

Enterprise authorization still needs to determine whether that authority applies to the action currently being attempted.

> **Assigned authority and effective authority are not necessarily the same thing.**

## 5. Use multiple authorization models together

I do not view RBAC and ABAC as competing approaches where one needs to replace the other.

Mature authorization architectures can combine multiple mechanisms because they solve different aspects of the problem.

**RBAC — broad responsibility**

> This subject is an Approver.

**ABAC — attribute-based constraints**

> This subject belongs to Business A and may operate Account X.

**Policy — contextual rules**

> An approver cannot approve a transaction they initiated.

**Limits — quantitative authority**

> This subject may approve transactions up to 500,000.

**Mandates — business authority**

> Two authorised approvers are required.

**Relationship — authority over a resource**

> This subject acts on behalf of Organisation Y.

Together:

These mechanisms solve different parts of the authorization problem.

> **Treating them as interchangeable can create unnecessary complexity.**

The objective is not to select the most sophisticated authorization model.

It is to use the appropriate mechanism for each dimension of authority.

## 6. The subject is not the customer record

Enterprise environments often contain many identifiers:

```text
Authentication Subject
Customer Identifier
Business Identifier
Employee Identifier
Account Identifier
Relationship Identifier
Correlation Identifier
```

These identifiers represent different concepts.

They should not be treated as interchangeable.

Authorization requires a stable representation of the **subject making the request**.

That subject can then be related to customers, organisations, accounts or other resources.

My principle is:

> **Identity, customer identity and business relationship are related concepts, but they should not be collapsed into a single identifier.**

This distinction also allows an authenticated subject to exist before every downstream business identifier or relationship has necessarily been established.

The authorization model remains centred on the subject while relationships to business resources are managed independently.

## 7. Resource scope matters

A permission without scope may be significantly broader than intended.

For example:

`ACCOUNT_VIEW`

should not automatically mean:

> View every account.

Likewise:

`PAYMENT_APPROVE`

should not automatically mean:

> Approve every payment.

Authority frequently requires both a permission and the resources to which that permission applies.

<ArchitectureFlow
  steps={['Permission', 'Resource Scope']}
  direction="vertical"
  join="plus"
  result="Effective Authority"
  label="Permission plus resource scope"
/>

For example:

```text
ACCOUNT_VIEW
     │
     ▼
  Business A
     │
     ├── Account 1
     └── Account 3
```

This is where attributes and business relationships become particularly important.

The permission describes **what** the subject may potentially do.

Scope constrains **where that authority applies**.

## 8. Context changes the answer

The same subject may perform the same action against the same resource and still receive a different authorization decision depending on context.

```text
Same Subject
Same Action
Same Resource
Different Context
      ↓
Different Decision
```

Context might include:

- transaction amount;
- authentication strength;
- channel;
- jurisdiction;
- time;
- device trust;
- transaction state;
- risk indicators;
- initiator identity.

This is why static permissions alone are not always sufficient.

Authorization may need to evaluate the conditions under which authority is being exercised, not simply whether authority was previously assigned.

## 9. ALLOW and DENY are sometimes insufficient

Traditional authorization is often represented as:

```text
ALLOW
DENY
```

For many use cases, that remains appropriate.

But enterprise processes may require additional conditions before an action can proceed.

Examples include:

```text
STEP_UP_REQUIRED
APPROVAL_REQUIRED
ADDITIONAL_AUTHORIZATION_REQUIRED
LIMIT_EXCEEDED
```

I prefer to distinguish the **decision**, the **obligations associated with that decision**, and the **reason**.

For example:

```text
Decision:
    ALLOW | DENY

Obligations:
    STEP_UP
    REQUIRE_APPROVAL
    REQUIRE_SECOND_AUTHORIZER

Reason:
    LIMIT_EXCEEDED
    MANDATE_NOT_SATISFIED
    SOD_VIOLATION
```

This distinction prevents every business condition from becoming a new decision type.

My principle is:

> **Authorization should be able to express not only whether an action is permitted, but what conditions must be satisfied before it can proceed.**

The exact decision model may vary by domain, but the distinction between outcome, obligation and reason is valuable.

## 10. Separate policy decision from enforcement

A reusable authorization architecture benefits from separating the point at which a decision is made from the point at which it is enforced.

Conceptually:

    'Decision',
    'PEP — Enforcement',
  ]}
  sources={['Policies', 'Attributes (PIP)', 'Policy Administration (PAP)']}
  sourcesLabel="Architectural roles supporting the decision"
  label="Policy enforcement and decision"

The standard architectural concepts are:

**PEP — Policy Enforcement Point**  
Intercepts the action and enforces the authorization decision.

**PDP — Policy Decision Point**  
Evaluates the authorization request and produces the decision.

**PIP — Policy Information Point**  
Provides attributes required during policy evaluation.

**PAP — Policy Administration Point**  
Supports policy definition, administration and lifecycle.

The value of this separation is architectural consistency.

> **Policy can evolve without requiring authorization logic to be repeatedly embedded throughout every consuming application.**

## 11. The hardest question: where do attributes come from?

The PDP requires information in order to make a decision.

The difficult architectural question is where that information comes from.

A simplistic implementation might synchronously retrieve everything:

<ArchitectureBranch
  root="PDP"
  branches={[
    'Identity',
    'Customer',
    'Relationship',
    'Account',
    'Mandate',
    'Limits',
    'Risk',
    'Policy',
  ]}
  label="Policy Information Problem — potential dependencies"
/>

The problem is that authorization then becomes dependent on the latency and availability of every downstream capability.

Decision latency increases.

Availability becomes constrained by multiple dependencies.

The authorization capability begins to behave like a distributed orchestration layer.

I think of this as the **Policy Information Problem**.

There are several broad approaches.

### A. Synchronous retrieval

Retrieve authoritative information when the decision is made.

This provides strong freshness but can introduce latency and availability dependencies.

### B. Caller-supplied context

Allow trusted context to accompany the authorization request.

This can improve performance, but the trust boundary becomes critical. The PDP must know which supplied attributes it is permitted to trust.

### C. Authorization projection

Maintain decision-relevant attributes closer to the authorization capability.

This can improve performance and resilience but introduces questions around freshness, synchronisation and consistency.

### D. Hybrid

Combine trusted request context, locally available decision information and selective authoritative retrieval based on the characteristics of the attribute.

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  The PDP should evaluate policy, not become an orchestration engine for the enterprise.
</PrincipleCallout>

The right information strategy depends on the correctness, freshness and availability requirements of each attribute.

## 12. Attribute freshness is part of authorization correctness

Caching authorization information is not merely a performance decision.

Consider what happens when:

- authority is revoked;
- a mandate changes;
- a relationship changes;
- an approval limit is reduced.

If authorization continues evaluating against stale information, authority that no longer exists may continue to be granted.

That means:

> **Authorization data has a correctness window, not merely a cache TTL.**

Different attributes can tolerate different levels of freshness.

For example:

```text
Policy definitions      → Version controlled
Role definitions        → Relatively stable
Relationship status     → Higher freshness requirement
Transaction amount      → Request-time context
Risk signal             → Potentially real-time
```

The architecture should therefore not apply one caching strategy indiscriminately to every authorization attribute.

Freshness is part of the authorization model itself.

## 13. Deny by default

The baseline remains simple.

If sufficient authority cannot be established:

```text
DENY
```

Not:

> “The system could not evaluate the request, therefore allow it.”

Missing information should not silently broaden authority.

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Uncertainty in authorization should reduce authority, not expand it.
</PrincipleCallout>

That principle should influence policy design, failure handling and dependency behaviour.

## 14. Authorization decisions must be explainable

A decision should not simply produce:

```json
{
  "decision": "DENY"
}
```

For operational, governance and audit purposes, the organisation may need additional decision evidence:

```text
Decision
Reason
Policy
Policy Version
Relevant Context
Obligations
Correlation
Timestamp
```

Not all of this information should necessarily be returned to the end user.

But the organisation should be able to answer questions such as:

> Why was this action permitted?

or:

> Why was this action denied?

My principle is:

> **If an authorization decision affects significant business activity, the organisation should be able to explain how that decision was reached.**

Explainability is therefore not simply an operational convenience.

It is part of designing authorization as a governable enterprise capability.

## 15. Audit the decision without creating a surveillance database

Authorization decisions generate valuable audit evidence.

But indiscriminately logging every token, attribute and request payload creates additional security and privacy risk.

An authorization audit may need to capture:

- subject reference;
- action;
- resource reference;
- decision;
- reason;
- policy version;
- relevant decision context;
- correlation identifier;
- timestamp.

It should avoid unnecessarily storing:

- raw access tokens;
- unnecessary personal information;
- secrets;
- complete sensitive payloads.

My principle is:

> **Authorization audit should preserve decision evidence without unnecessarily duplicating sensitive data.**

Auditability and data minimisation should coexist.

## 16. Centralise decision consistency, not every piece of data

This connects directly to the platform architecture discussion.

A reusable authorization capability does not require centralising all user, customer, account, mandate and transaction information.

We may choose to centralise concerns such as:

- decision semantics;
- policy evaluation;
- permission taxonomy;
- policy governance;
- audit expectations.

While authoritative business information remains within the domains responsible for it.

Conceptually:

<ArchitectureBranch
  root="Authorization Policy"
  branches={['Identity Domain', 'Business Domain', 'Resource Domain']}
  label="Centralise authorization, not domain data"
/>

My principle is:

> **Centralising authorization does not require centralising the data used to make authorization decisions.**

That is an important platform boundary.

The authorization capability needs access to decision-relevant information.

It does not necessarily need to become the system of record for that information.

## 17. Authorization itself is a platform capability

This is where the broader architecture perspectives connect.

A mature enterprise authorization capability can provide:

- consistent decision semantics;
- policy evaluation;
- a common permission vocabulary;
- reusable enforcement patterns;
- auditability;
- governance;
- decision interfaces;
- policy lifecycle management.

Without this consistency, applications may independently implement logic such as:

```text
if user.role == ...
if amount < ...
if business == ...
if approver != initiator ...
```

Over time, authorization policy becomes fragmented across applications, APIs and user interfaces.

The same business rule may be implemented differently in multiple places.

But the opposite extreme is also problematic.

A central authorization capability should not attempt to own every piece of domain authority or business logic.

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Centralise the decision framework and reusable policy concerns. Keep domain authority with the domain that owns it.
</PrincipleCallout>

This preserves the value of a shared authorization capability without turning it into a centralised repository for every business rule in the enterprise.

## The role of Enterprise Architecture, as I see it

Enterprise authorization extends beyond Identity and Access Management.

Identity concerns authentication.

Security concerns access control.

Product teams understand business rules.

Platform teams care about reusable capabilities.

Domains own business authority.

All of these concerns intersect in authorization.

The Enterprise Architect's responsibility is not to define every authorization policy.

It is to help establish the boundaries.

Conceptually:

<ArchitectureFlow
  steps={['Identity', 'Subject', 'Entitlement + Context', 'Policy', 'Decision', 'Enforcement']}
  direction="vertical"
  label="Enterprise authorization boundary"
/>

The architecture needs to make clear:

- who owns identity;
- who owns relationships;
- who owns entitlements;
- who owns domain attributes;
- who defines policy;
- who evaluates policy;
- who enforces decisions;
- how authorization is governed;
- how decisions are audited.

The objective is not to create the most sophisticated authorization engine possible.

It is to establish a model where authority can be **expressed clearly, evaluated consistently, enforced reliably and evolved without embedding policy throughout the enterprise**.

That is the outcome I care about.

Not whether the architecture can claim to use RBAC, ABAC or a particular policy engine.

Not how sophisticated the rules language is.

But whether the organisation can reliably answer a fundamental question:

> **May this subject perform this action against this resource under these conditions?**

And whether it can trust and explain the answer.

Because ultimately:

> **Enterprise authorization is not about roles, attributes or policy engines. It is about establishing who may do what, to which resource, under which conditions—and being able to trust and explain the decision.**
