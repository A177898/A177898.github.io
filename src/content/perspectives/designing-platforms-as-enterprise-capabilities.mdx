Enterprise Architecture places significant value on reuse.

When we find multiple teams building similar capabilities, fragmented technology implementations and repeated investment in solving the same problems, the instinct is understandable:

> **Should we build a common platform?**

Sometimes the answer is yes.

But there is a risk of reaching that conclusion too quickly.

Reuse alone does not make something a platform. Centralising technology does not automatically create enterprise value.

A shared service can still be difficult to consume. A common API can still create tight coupling. A centrally managed technology stack can become a bottleneck. And a platform depended upon by many teams can eventually represent a greater concentration of risk than the duplication it was intended to replace.

So I start with a different question:

> **Where does a shared capability create greater enterprise leverage than the dependency it introduces?**

That changes the conversation.

The objective is no longer:

> “How do we centralise more?”

It becomes:

> “Where should we create reusable capability, and how do we design it so that teams can consume it without inheriting unnecessary complexity?”

That, for me, is the distinction between **shared technology** and an **enterprise platform capability**.

## 1. A platform is not defined by its technology

The term *platform* is used extensively.

Infrastructure platforms. API platforms. Data platforms. Identity platforms. Developer platforms.

Sometimes the term is appropriate.

Sometimes it simply describes technology that several teams happen to use.

I do not think that is sufficient.

The technology matters, but it is the implementation mechanism.

The capability is what the enterprise actually consumes.

<PrincipleCallout>
  A platform is not defined by what it runs on. It becomes a platform when multiple consumers can independently use its capabilities to create value.
</PrincipleCallout>

## 2. Not everything reusable should become a platform

Once duplication is identified, there is often pressure to centralise it:

> “Several teams have built this. Let's build it once.”

That may sound efficient.

But platformisation is not free.

A shared capability needs ownership, a roadmap, security, operations, support, lifecycle management, funding and a mechanism for prioritising the needs of multiple consumers.

It also creates dependency.

Once multiple products depend on the same capability, changing that capability can affect all of them.

The question therefore cannot simply be:

> “Can this be reused?”

It needs to become:

> **“Does making this a shared platform capability create enough enterprise value to justify the dependency and operating model it introduces?”**

My principle is:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Reuse alone is not sufficient reason to platformise. A platform should exist where shared capability creates greater enterprise leverage than the coupling it introduces.
</PrincipleCallout>

Some capabilities should be shared.

Some should remain within products or domains.

Others are sufficiently commoditised that they may be better purchased or consumed.

Enterprise Architecture should make that distinction deliberately.

## 3. Start with capability, not the platform

A common mistake is to begin with the technology category:

> “We need an API platform.”

> “We need a data platform.”

At that point, we risk selecting the solution before clearly defining the problem.

I prefer to work in the opposite direction:

Start with:

> **What capability needs to be consistently available to multiple consumers?**

Then determine what belongs within that capability, what remains outside it, who consumes it, how they consume it and what guarantees those consumers require.

Only then should technology become the primary discussion.

This helps prevent us from searching for problems that fit a platform or technology we have already selected.

## 4. A shared service is not automatically a platform

A shared service can be valuable. It allows more than one consumer to use the same functionality.

But something changes as adoption grows.

A typical shared service may provide functionality where reuse is possible, integration is negotiated, onboarding is relatively manual and change remains largely provider-driven.

A platform capability is different.

It is designed for **repeatable consumption**.

Reuse is intentional. Contracts are explicit. The capability is consumer-oriented. Self-service is provided where appropriate. Compatibility becomes an explicit concern. The consumption experience itself is designed.

Not every shared service needs to become a platform.

But once multiple teams begin depending on it, **consumption and evolution become first-class architectural concerns**.

That is the threshold I care about.

## 5. Boundaries matter

Platforms have a natural tendency to grow.

A useful shared capability attracts consumers. Those consumers introduce new requirements. Related responsibilities begin accumulating.

Eventually:

<ArchitectureFlow
  steps={['EVERYTHING', 'GIANT PLATFORM', 'EVERYONE']}
  direction="vertical"
  label="Platform growth anti-pattern"
/>

Instead of reducing coupling, we have centralised it.

Every significant change requires coordination. Every new requirement expands the platform's responsibilities.

A platform therefore needs clear answers to several questions:

- What capability do we own?
- What information do we own?
- What decisions belong within this boundary?
- What explicitly does **not** belong here?
- What contracts do we expose?
- Where should consumers retain autonomy?

The objective is not to create one platform that owns everything.

It is to establish a coherent ecosystem of capabilities with clear responsibilities.

<PrincipleCallout>
  A platform should reduce coupling for its consumers, not concentrate coupling inside a new enterprise bottleneck.
</PrincipleCallout>

## 6. The consumer is part of the architecture

It is possible to build something that is technically reusable but practically difficult to consume.

The API exists.

Documentation exists somewhere.

Access can be requested.

Credentials can eventually be provisioned after several interactions.

Technically, the capability is reusable.

Architecturally, I would argue that it is not working effectively as a platform.

My test is:

> **If the cost of consuming a capability approaches the cost of rebuilding it, it is not meaningfully reusable.**

The consumer experience therefore needs to be designed.

That includes:

- discoverability;
- clear documentation;
- efficient onboarding;
- explicit contracts;
- authentication and authorisation;
- test or sandbox capabilities where appropriate;
- predictable error behaviour;
- observability;
- versioning;
- support;
- self-service;
- feedback mechanisms.

This is more than developer experience.

If the capability is difficult to consume, teams will find alternatives.

Duplication returns.

The platform objective fails even if the underlying technology is technically sound.

## 7. Platforms need contracts

Consumers should not need to understand the internal implementation of a platform in order to use it safely.

They need to understand its **contract**.

That contract operates across several dimensions:

  root="Platform Contract"
  branches={[
    'Functional',
    'Interface',
    'Information',
    'Security',
    'Availability / SLO',
    'Versioning',
    'Lifecycle / Support',
  ]}
  label="Platform contract dimensions"
/>

**Functional:** what the capability does.

**Interface:** how it is consumed.

**Information:** the meaning and ownership of information crossing the boundary.

**Security:** how trust is established and maintained.

**Availability / SLO:** what consumers can reasonably expect.

**Versioning:** how the capability evolves.

**Lifecycle / Support:** how the capability is supported, deprecated and eventually retired.

> **Good platform architecture minimises what a consumer needs to know about the platform's internal implementation.**

Consumers depend on capability and contract, not implementation.

That gives both sides greater freedom.

Consumers can evolve without understanding platform internals, while the platform can change its implementation without unnecessarily disrupting every consumer.

## 8. Reuse and autonomy need to coexist

Enterprise Architecture naturally values standardisation.

And for good reason.

Standardisation can reduce duplication, strengthen security and simplify operations.

But excessive standardisation can unnecessarily constrain teams.

The question should therefore not be:

> “How do we make everyone use the central platform?”

It should be:

> **“Where does enterprise consistency create greater value than local autonomy?”**

Some capabilities benefit significantly from consistency.

Others sit closer to the areas where products and domains need to differentiate.

I think about this as two conceptual zones:

The boundary will move depending on the capability.

Too little standardisation creates duplication.

Too much creates bottlenecks.

Architecture is responsible for finding the appropriate balance.

> **Platform strategy is partly the discipline of deciding where the enterprise should standardise and where teams should remain free to differentiate.**

## 9. Success creates critical dependencies

Reuse creates leverage.

It also concentrates risk.

If one product depends on a service and that service fails, the impact may be relatively contained.

If many products depend on the same platform and it fails, the conversation changes significantly.

> **The value of a platform increases with adoption. So does the consequence of its failure.**

As adoption grows, expectations increase around:

- availability;
- resilience;
- capacity;
- disaster recovery;
- observability;
- security;
- backward compatibility;
- incident management;
- operational support.

These concerns need to be considered early.

An enterprise-critical capability should not continue to be operated as though it were the small shared service it may originally have been.

A useful question to ask early is:

> **What does this capability need to become if it succeeds?**

That does not mean over-engineering on day one.

It means understanding the architectural consequences of successful adoption before they become operational surprises.

## 10. Ownership matters more than technology

A platform without durable ownership eventually becomes shared technical debt.

Someone needs to own more than the code.

Ownership needs to cover areas such as:

- direction;
- roadmap;
- consumer needs;
- contracts;
- security;
- service levels;
- operations;
- lifecycle;
- adoption;
- compatibility;
- deprecation.

This is where platform thinking intersects with product thinking.

A platform has consumers.

Those consumers have competing needs.

The capability needs to evolve.

> **If we cannot identify who owns the evolution of a shared capability, we do not have a sustainable platform. We have created a dependency without sustainable ownership.**

The ownership model does not need to be identical for every platform.

But accountability needs to be explicit.

## 11. Governance should enable, not become the bottleneck

Shared capabilities require governance.

Without it, contracts drift, standards diverge and consumers begin using the capability in inconsistent ways.

But governance becomes counterproductive when every use of the platform requires central approval.

At that point, we may have created a platform intended to accelerate delivery and surrounded it with a process that slows its consumers down.

I therefore distinguish between **guardrails** and **gatekeeping**.

Guardrails establish:

- supported patterns;
- security requirements;
- interface standards;
- lifecycle expectations;
- compatibility rules;
- service expectations.

Within those boundaries, teams should be able to move independently wherever possible.

A useful test is:

> **If every successful use of the platform requires the platform team to be directly involved, the capability is not yet operating at platform scale.**

The objective is **governed autonomy**.

## 12. Measure the platform by what changes for consumers

A platform is not successful simply because it exists.

Success is demonstrated by what the enterprise can do differently because the platform exists.

I look for evidence such as:

- Are multiple consumers adopting the capability?
- Is duplicated implementation reducing?
- Is onboarding becoming easier?
- Can consumers evolve more independently?
- Are standards becoming more consistent?
- Is delivery becoming faster or simpler?
- Can redundant implementations be retired?

If teams consistently avoid the platform, I would not immediately conclude that the teams are the problem.

Perhaps the platform is difficult to consume.

Perhaps the capability boundary is wrong.

Perhaps it solves a problem those consumers do not actually have.

> **Adoption is architectural feedback. We should learn from it.**

And ultimately:

<PrincipleCallout>
  Platform success is demonstrated by what consumers can do differently, not by the existence of the platform itself.
</PrincipleCallout>

## 13. Design to evolve

Platforms will not exist forever in their current form.

Technology changes.

Capabilities evolve.

Better implementation options emerge.

Some capabilities become commodities.

Others become increasingly strategic.

The architecture should therefore allow the implementation behind the capability to change.

This is why boundaries and contracts matter.

If consumers are tightly coupled to internal implementation details, replacing the platform may itself become a major transformation programme.

If consumers depend primarily on stable contracts, the enterprise retains more options.

> **The strongest platform boundary is one that can survive even when the technology behind it changes.**

The implementation does not need to last forever.

The capability boundary should be designed with evolution in mind.

> “Several teams need this, therefore it should become a platform.”

</PrincipleCallout>

## The role of Enterprise Architecture, as I see it

Enterprise Architecture should not own every platform.

Nor should Enterprise Architecture determine every implementation detail within them.

Our role is to help determine **where platformisation creates enterprise leverage**.

At a high level:

Some capabilities should remain close to the products, journeys or domains where differentiation matters.

Some create greater value when consistently available to multiple consumers and are therefore candidates for platformisation.

Others are sufficiently commoditised that they may be better purchased or consumed as utilities.

The question is not:

> “How many platforms can we build?”

It is:

> **“Where does a shared capability allow the enterprise to move faster without introducing unnecessary constraint?”**

Answering that requires strategy, economics, technology, organisational design and operating model to be considered together.

It also requires discipline about what **not** to platformise.

## Platforms are a means, not the destination

Platforms are not the architectural destination.

They are a mechanism for allowing the enterprise to achieve more without requiring every part of the organisation to build every capability independently.

That requires more than shared technology.

It requires clear capability boundaries, explicit contracts, a well-designed consumption experience, sustainable ownership, appropriate governance and an operating model capable of supporting the dependency being created.

It also requires recognising that centralisation is not inherently better.

The value comes from **leverage**.

A well-designed platform should make the enterprise easier to change, not harder.

It should allow teams to focus their effort on the capabilities that differentiate them while relying on shared capabilities where consistency and reuse create greater value.

That is the outcome I care about.

Not how many platforms the organisation has.

Not how much technology has been centralised.

But the leverage those platforms create.

> **Platforms are not the goal. The goal is enabling the enterprise to do more without requiring every part of it to build everything for itself.**
